A memory controller's busy fraction, read on one card, ranks five settings it never saw,
and under-predicts the one magnitude it was asked for by half

Read as mem_busy on one card; checked against an H200, a B300, a PRO 6000 and a second H100, and against a prediction committed before the run

A decode step on Qwen3-8B keeps one card's memory controller busy 87 % of the time; on gemma-4-26B-A4B, 38 %. Read that number on one card. It predicts which of the two gains more from a faster card, from a second card, or from faster memory, and which loses more to slower memory — on hardware it was never measured on. It does not say by how much, and a prediction committed before the data says so.

Abstract

Every projection in this repository was built from two Radeons under a desk and whatever Colab offered. The rented time added six machine configurations on the decode and prefill ladder — H100, H200, B300, RTX PRO 6000, two H100s, two PRO 6000s — and eight on the collective. The run produced 586 ladder measurements and 385 collective cells with 0 errors, pushing context past 32 000 tokens for the first time here. Two platform controls come first. An A100 and an L4 rented on the same platform reproduce Colab's committed rows to 0.07 % and 0.9 %, which lets each ratio below be read as a card difference.

The finding is one counter. In campaign-2026-09-02d, the memory controller's busy fraction on one card (mem_busy) decided what a second Radeon was worth: 90 % and 1.70× on the bf16 8B, 56 % and 1.20× on the w4a16 12B. That was five cells on one box. The counter now orders the outcome across five settings: one on the pair, and four against an H100 baseline. Those four are an H200, a B300, a PRO 6000 — the other direction, because its memory is slower — and a second H100 over NVLink, which is worth 2.9 % to the model that reads least. It does not predict magnitude. A prediction for the H200 was committed before that run. The ordering held at both ends, but the spread came back nearly double. At the memory-bound end, the gain exceeded the memory-clock ratio itself, which the formula used cannot produce. Ordinal, not cardinal, and on the record as such.

The wire is not the axis, and now that is measured. Across eight configurations the collective's bandwidth end spans 62× and its latency end 3.2×. Batch-1 decode reduces one row and lands on the latency end. NVLink is worth 20 % at two cards and 171 % at four. Three further findings emerged. Four cards chose three attention backends with no flag set. A $7.10/h B300 loses to a $3.95/h H100 on the MoE, but wins by 66 % on the 8B. And past 32 000 tokens, a bounded attention window flattens the decode curve rather than a recurrent state — which falsified a prediction written into the runner before the run.

1Two controls, before any ratio

Every ratio below divides a card rented on Modal by an A100 or an L4 measured on Colab in August. Cross-platform comparisons assume the two environments behave alike. To test that assumption directly, the same two cards were rented on Modal and run on the same models at the context budget and ladder Colab used.

2One counter orders five settings

The metric mem_busy_pct records the fraction of samples in which the memory controller was busy. The same sampler reads it on every machine (harness/telemetry.py: amdgpu's mem_busy_percent on Radeons, NVML's utilization.memory on CUDA). It represents a busy fraction rather than achieved bandwidth — the harness marks achieved bandwidth absent on both platforms and explains why. On 2026-09-02, reading it on one Radeon across two models decided what the second card was worth. 3And sizes none of them

An ordering across five settings is a correlation until it predicts something. An H200 provides the cleanest test: it matches the H100 in compute capability, 700 W cap, and SM clock ceiling, differing only in faster memory. If a decode step spends a fraction f of its time waiting on memory and the rest on everything else, multiplying memory speed by r gives 1 / ((1 − f) + f / r). Here f was taken as mem_busy on the H100 baseline. The ratio r came from the two cards' measured maximum memory clocks — 2 619 against 3 201 MHz, so 1.222 — rather than vendor bandwidth figures unmeasured by this repository. The six predictions were committed before the run.

4Why the second card's value is not on the wire

The a100 article's section 9 used to say the second Radeon's bandwidth was spent on the interconnect. Campaign allreduce-2026-09-02 withdrew that claim with direct measurements on the pair. Under graph replay, a collective took 16.6–21.5 µs — roughly a tenth of a step — paid equally by a model the second card helps 1.70× and one it helps 1.20×. The same sweep across seven NVIDIA configurations gives the withdrawal a mechanism.

5Four cards, three attention backends, nobody asked for any of them

One vLLM, one command line, no backend flag anywhere in the sweep. The runner reads the backend directly out of every serve log rather than trusting a table, which is the only reason this split is known. Because vLLM 0.28 has no flag for the decoder's backend (attention_backend exists only in config/speculative.py, read out of the installed package), the control that would separate the card from the kernel is not available.

6The newest card is not the fastest card

7Past 32 000: what makes a curve flat

Every ladder in this repository stopped at 32 000 tokens until this sweep, where the H100 carried five models to 128 000. A prediction was written into the runner before the run. The models featured three distinct attention designs: gemma-4 reads a KV cache that grows without bound; Qwen3.8-27B carries a recurrent state that does not grow at all; and Muse-Glimmer-30B attends through a 2 048-token window that grows and then stops. The prediction placed Muse-Glimmer between the two.

8What is not established

The counter's magnitude

Section 3 has the limit. The form 1 / ((1 − f) + f / r), with f read off one card, under-predicts the spread by half and cannot produce the memory-bound end at all. Either the effective bandwidth ratio exceeds the memory-clock ratio — the vendors' figures give 1.43, at which the same form over-predicts every row — or the H200 differs from the H100 in something the prediction assumed away. Nothing here separates those.

A backend control

Figure 6's B300 column and every gemma-4 comparison conflate the card with its backend. Because vLLM 0.28 cannot fix the decoder backend, this was not measured.

Two configurations, by arithmetic and by budget

gemma-4-26B-A4B cannot run at TP=4: CompressedTensors WNA16 MoE with static group scales requires the MoE intermediate size per tensor-parallel partition to be divisible by group_size (32) — a property of the checkpoint, not a failure to retry. The other four models at TP=4, and Muse-Glimmer at TP=2 on the rented pairs, were not bought.

Achieved bandwidth, and one counter on two vendors

mem_busy is a busy fraction: 87 % at one rate is not 87 % at another, and achieved bandwidth is absent on both platforms. The counter travels; its denominator does not. It also spans two driver metrics: amdgpu's mem_busy_percent on Radeons and NVML's utilization.memory on CUDA. That the same two models land at 90/56 on one and 87/53 on the other is evidence the two counters measure alike. It is not proof.

Four of the five settings share a baseline

The H200, B300, PRO 6000, and second-H100 rows all divide by one H100 sitting, so any error there shifts four rows together. Platform controls bound that sitting against Colab to 0.07 %, and the pair ran on separate hardware. The five are one independent check on the pair's finding, not five.

Where the dropped prefill rungs sit

Twenty-three prefill cells failed the repeatability cut on the rented cards, all of them between 500 and 2 000 tokens, on the cold engine's first requests. Two decode cells failed it too, both on the H200: gemma-4-26B-A4B at 1 000 and gemma-4-31B at 500. Nothing above depends on them.
