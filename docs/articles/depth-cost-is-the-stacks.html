2× RX 7900 XT · six checkpoints to 128 000 tokens · three software stacks · one checkpoint measured on both attention backends

The flattest model by retention is the steepest by cost,
and one serve flag moves it three places

What a “loses 28 % to 96 000” figure is made of, on a machine where the same weights span 3.00× of depth cost without changing

Six checkpoints ran the same sixteen-rung ladder on the same two cards. Ranked by what each keeps of its own 500-token rate, Qwen3.8-27B is the flattest of them. Ranked by what each pays per token of context, it is the steepest. Both numbers are correct and they are not about the same thing — and the second one moves by 3.00× on this checkpoint when nothing but the software changes.

2× RX 7900 XT · gfx1100 · TP=2 vLLM 0.27.1.dev5 · ROCm 10.0 campaign 2026-09-07

Abstract

A long-context benchmark usually reports retention: what fraction of its short-prompt rate a model keeps at depth. On the six arms of campaign-2026-09-03 that number ranks the models in almost the reverse of the order they pay in. The reason is arithmetic rather than subtle: a retention percentage divides the depth cost by the baseline, and across these six the baselines span 8.4×. The slowest model in the set is the one that looks most depth-robust.

The alternative is not safe either. Fitting milliseconds per token against context gives a slope in microseconds per context token, which is a cost rather than a ratio and does not carry the baseline. On this pair that slope is a better description — and it is still not a property of the model. The same Qwen3.8-27B checkpoint, on the same two cards with the same weights and the same upstream patch, measures 0.350, 0.233 and 0.117 µs per context token on three software stacks that differ by a vLLM version and one serve flag. That is 3.00×, and it moves the checkpoint from last place in this set to third.

The backend half of that is an attribution, not a comparison. The two attention backends were measured in two sessions of one run, and a third session repeated the first arm to price the session boundary: 0.50 % at worst, against a gap that reaches 48 %. Their intercepts agree to 1.5 % while their slopes differ by 2.11×, so the flag moves the depth term and leaves the baseline where it was. What it costs is prefill: the decode advantage widens to 1.48× at 128 000 tokens while prefill falls to 0.44×, and neither has turned over at the deepest rung this machine can hold.

1Two numbers, and they disagree

Every arm below is the same ladder on the same two cards, TP=2, batch 1, from campaign-2026-09-03. Their deepest rungs differ — 32 000 for the 8B, 128 000 for three of the others — so both columns are computed on the span every arm has, 500 to 32 000. A slope taken over a longer span is a different number, and comparing one to the other would be comparing curvature.

2What the percentage is dividing by

The inversion is not a paradox and not a measurement error. Retention is 1 − cost × depth / baseline, so an arm with a large baseline hides a large cost. The intercepts of these six fits — what a decode step costs before any context is read — span , from the MoE's fastest step to the hybrid SSM's slowest. Over 32 000 tokens the hybrid pays the most per token of anything here, and it is still only an eighth of a step it was already spending. It looks robust because it is slow.

That is worth stating plainly, because the reading it invites is the opposite one: a retention percentage rewards a slow baseline. Two models can have identical depth behaviour and different retention, and two models with identical retention can differ by several times in what depth actually costs them.

3The same weights, three stacks

If the slope is the better number, the next question is whose property it is. This checkpoint has now been measured on three software stacks with the weights, the cards, the parallelism, the ladder and the vllm#45916 split-KV patch all held: vLLM 0.23.1 on ROCm 7.14, which is the arm the campaign published; vLLM 0.27.1 on ROCm 10.0, which is campaign-2026-09-06; and the same 0.27.1 with --attention-backend TRITON_ATTN, which is campaign-2026-09-07.

Nothing about the model changed between those three lines. The span is , and it is enough to move this checkpoint from the bottom of Figure 1's cost ranking to third — past the MoE, and within a whisker of Qwen3-8B, which is a quarter of its size.

4Why the last step is the backend and not the session

A backend is chosen when the server starts, so the two 0.27 arms cannot share a session, and an A–B difference is a backend difference only if the session boundary costs less than it does. This repository has been caught by exactly that before: rccl-ndebug-ab-2026-09-04 published a 3.6 % arm effect that turned out to be session order. So the run has a third session, repeating the first arm at three rungs after the second one finished.

Two further things make the pair an attribution rather than a race. The intercepts of the two fits agree to 1.5 % while the slopes differ by 2.11×: the flag moves what a token of context costs and leaves what a step costs alone. And the direction reverses in prefill, which is the shape of a trade rather than of one arm simply being better.

5The trade has not turned over

campaign-2026-09-02c measured these two backends to 32 000 tokens and found them trading, with both gaps still widening where it stopped. At four times that depth they are still widening: decode reaches and prefill at 128 000. Neither backend is the better one. They divide one cost between prefill and decode, and which division a deployment wants is a question about its traffic.

6The arm whose curve is not a line

One row of Figure 1 carries a warning rather than a measurement. Muse-Glimmer-30B fits a line at , where every other arm is above . Its attention runs through a 2 048-token window, so its cost per token stops growing rather than growing more slowly, and a straight line cannot say that. Its slope is listed because removing it would be worse than printing it beside a caveat, but it is not the same kind of number as the others and it should not be ranked against them.

That is also the case for reporting r² next to any slope of this kind: it is what tells a reader whether the single number in front of them describes the curve it was fitted to.

7What to report instead

Two numbers rather than one, and a third that says whether they mean anything: the intercept, which is what a decode step costs before context; the slope, which is what each token of context adds; and r², which says whether the fit is a description or a summary. A retention percentage is derivable from the first two at any depth, while neither is derivable from it.

None of this makes the slope a property of a model. On this machine it is a property of a model and the kernel that serves it, and the second term is the larger one: from software against the 2.7× that separates the six different checkpoints in Figure 1's cost column.

8What is not established

  • Only one checkpoint was measured on both backends. Whether the other five would move the same way is not known, and they cannot be re-run together on 0.27 in any case: gemma-4's Quark plugin reads a heterogeneous head_dim and dies before loading.
  • Nothing here opens either kernel. That prefill moves the other way is the shape of a trade, not an explanation of one.
  • Every cell is batch 1. max-num-seqs is 16 for capacity, not for load. What either backend does under concurrency at depth is a different measurement.
  • The six arms of Figure 1 are one machine. The claim about what a percentage divides by is arithmetic and travels; the particular ordering is this pair's.
