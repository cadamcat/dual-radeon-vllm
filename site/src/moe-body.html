RDNA3 · MoE · torch.compile · measured on 2× RX 7900 XT

The fastest model here
was written off at 15 tok/s

An earlier probe gave torch.compile twenty minutes, ran out of patience, added --enforce-eager, and recorded about 15 tok/s. "MoE is impractical on ROCm" went into this repository's own documents. The compile needs twenty-six minutes. Compiled, the same model is the fastest of five architectures measured on this machine.

2× RX 7900 XT · gfx1100 · TP=2 gemma-4-26B-A4B · 128 experts Zen 1 host · 8 cores measured 2026-07-25

Abstract

Compiled, a 128-expert MoE decodes at 107.82 tok/s at 500 tokens of context and 72.80 at 32000, ahead of every dense model measured here including ones with more parameters. Under --enforce-eager the same model was recorded at about 15, and that number is what the verdict was written from.

The cost of not using eager is one engine start of 1569 seconds, of which 54 is loading weights. It is single-threaded on an eight-core host because vllm/env_override.py assigns TORCHINDUCTOR_COMPILE_THREADS = "1" on import — a plain assignment, so exporting the variable yourself does nothing.

The 7.2× is not the damage. Eager also produced two qualitative phenomena that were read as properties of the architecture: a 131 W / 65 W split between the cards, taken as expert imbalance, and a decode rate independent of context, taken as a hybrid-like advantage. Compiled, the 22 measured rows are symmetric to within 2.26 % with identical VRAM on both cards, and the rate falls by a third across the context ladder.

Both wrong conclusions were published in this repository before they were withdrawn. The contribution here is two lines upstream — vllm#53891 and vllm#53892 — which keep the default of one thread and let a deliberate override through.

1What is being measured

One campaign, 2026-07-25, seven engine starts and 146 timed generations across five models and two parallelism settings. Decode rate is reported by the server over a generation of several hundred tokens after a warm-up; each cell is the mean of two rounds. Board power is sampled during each generation and recorded per card as the maximum seen in that window, alongside per-card VRAM.

init_engine_s is engine initialisation, not compile time. It covers loading weights, profiling, allocating the KV cache, torch.compile and graph capture. The weight-loading share is recorded separately, which is what lets Figure 2 show that on the two slow starts it is not where the time goes. Nothing here isolates Inductor's own share of the remainder.

The eager numbers are not in this repository Every compiled figure below is recomputed from benchmarks/results.jsonl. The eager measurements are not: they come from an earlier probe whose raw output was never committed, and they survive only as prose in docs/benchmarks.md. The article extracts them from that document rather than restating them, so the two cannot drift apart, but extraction is not the same as recomputation and the figures say so where it applies. The number that decided an architecture verdict is the one with the weakest provenance in this article.

2The verdict that got published

The probe recorded the model as "torch.compile did not finish in 20 minutes → impractical → forced --enforce-eager → ~15 tok/s", plus "severely asymmetric power draw (131 W / 65 W), the MoE fails to load both cards evenly". It was filed under architectures held back by ROCm, and the repository's architecture table read "mediocre, ~15 tok/s" for months.

Nothing about that reasoning is unusual. A compile that has not finished in twenty minutes looks like a compile that is not going to finish, and eager is the documented way past it. The step that does not survive is the one after: treating what eager then reports as a measurement of the architecture.

The 12B dense model repeated the mistake independently. An earlier context sweep used --enforce-eager to dodge the same compile wall and produced "a flat 15.8 tok/s, independent of context, with power bouncing between the cards". Compiled it is 59.91, and the flatness is gone.

3Where twenty-six minutes goes

The reason it is one core is a single line, run on every import vllm:

vllm/env_override.py:105 · still this on main, checked 2026-08-29
os.environ["TORCHINDUCTOR_COMPILE_THREADS"] = "1"

It is an assignment rather than a setdefault, so a value exported in the environment is overwritten before Inductor reads it. Importing vLLM leaves torch._inductor.config.compile_threads == 1 whatever the caller asked for. Inductor's own default would be one worker per core.

The default itself is not the problem Single-threaded compilation is deliberate upstream, for reasons recorded in vllm#10480 and vllm#10619: compile subprocesses interact badly with debuggers and with some launch paths. Changing that default is a different argument from the one made here. What is being reported is narrower — that the mechanism gives the operator no way to opt out, and that this is almost certainly not intended, since a plain assignment is what a setdefault looks like when nobody has needed the override yet.

4The two things eager invented

A 7.2× throughput loss is a bad measurement and a reader can discount it. The harder failure is that eager produced two qualitative results, each of which reads as a fact about the model rather than about the flag.

The power reading had an explanation ready, and it was wrong. Under pure tensor parallelism vLLM keeps all experts on every GPU and shards each expert's weights along the intermediate dimension, so compute is symmetric whatever the router does. Routing imbalance is a property of expert parallelism, which is not what was running. The asymmetry disappearing under compilation is what confirms it: nothing about the experts changed.

5What was sent upstream

Two lines, and they do not touch the default:

vllm#53892 · 2 insertions, 1 deletion, 1 file
-os.environ["TORCHINDUCTOR_COMPILE_THREADS"] = "1"
+os.environ.setdefault("TORCHINDUCTOR_COMPILE_THREADS", "1")
reported
vllm#53891, 2026-08-26, labelled rocm: an exported TORCHINDUCTOR_COMPILE_THREADS is silently overwritten.
proposed
vllm#53892, same day. The default of one is kept; only a deliberate export gets through.
verified
On the ROCm 7.14 / vLLM 0.23 image, importing vllm.env_override with the variable exported to 8 leaves compile_threads == 8; unset, it is 1 as before. Unpatched, the first case comes back clobbered.

6What is not established

That the compile output is usefully cached

The advice that goes with a 26-minute compile is "once, then cached", and this campaign does not support it. The only repeated start is the 12B at TP=1, where the cold side is 59.67 s and the warm side 33.36 s — a 1.79× saving on a start that was never the problem. Neither of the two configurations that took more than twenty minutes was ever started a second time here, so the warm cost of the expensive case is unmeasured. Compile caching is real and documented upstream; what is missing is a measurement of it on this host.

How much of the 1569 s is Inductor

init_engine_s covers loading, profiling, KV allocation, compilation and graph capture. Weight loading is separated out and is small. The rest is not decomposed, so "the compile takes 26 minutes" is shorthand for "engine init takes 26 minutes and it is not the weights".

The eager side of every comparison

The eager rates and the 131 W / 65 W reading were not recorded to a committed file. They are quoted from the document that carries them, and a re-run under --enforce-eager would be needed to put them on the same footing as everything else here. The direction is not in doubt; the exact ratio is one significant figure at best.

One host, and it is a slow one

A Zen 1 Threadripper with eight cores available to the container. The compile-thread argument scales with core count, and on a machine with many fast cores the same change is worth proportionally more. Nothing here measures the patched build's compile time, because the patch is upstream's to take.
